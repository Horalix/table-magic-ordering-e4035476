Thompson sampling built, switched off

Four data points should not outrank four hundred

The ranker scores each pairing by how often guests accept it. Today it uses the average of what it believes — and throws away how sure it is. So a pairing seen four times and one seen four hundred times get compared as though both numbers were facts.

Below, the wide curve is a pairing the system has barely seen; the narrow one it has seen four hundred times. Both are plausible around a quarter. Only one of them knows it.

PairingShownAcceptedScoresActually plausible
A · burger → new dessert440.2948% – 51%
B · burger → espresso4001000.24420% – 29%

A genuinely bad pairing — 2% accepted over 400 shows — wins 0.0% of impressions under the same sampler. Exploring costs nothing in exploitation. That is the second half of the test, and the half that is easy to forget.

It is written and switched off. A gate counts real service data — 2000 decisions, 15 pairings with fifty sightings each, at least one confirmed acceptance — and the nightly job turns sampling on the first night all three pass. Roughly three to four weeks of trade.

Expect a modest gain. Bandits pay off with many options and heavy traffic; a café with fifty menu items finds its good pairings a few weeks sooner and wastes fewer suggestions getting there. What made suggestions better is further down this page.

Market-basket lift two grains

Steak now, coffee at twenty to nine

The engine learns which dishes go together by looking at what arrives in the same basket. That works for the cart. It cannot work for the moment after the plates are cleared, because by then the steak was a different order, forty minutes ago.

Watch one table’s evening. Then watch the baskets change shape.

Both grains are kept. Order-level lift still drives the cart, where the basket really is the order. Visit-level lift drives the after-meal suggestion and the social proof, where the basket is the evening.

Honest by construction

A percentage is only shown from visit-grained data, above a support threshold, and quotes the lower bound of the interval rather than the flattering middle. Below that it says “often ordered with the steak”, or stays quiet. No invented scarcity, no countdowns — in a room where guests can see each other’s tables, a visibly false claim costs more trust than the upsell earns.

Unit of analysis the bug that flattered us

Three orders from one table is one observation

To prove the suggestions earn anything, one table in ten is shown none, and the two groups are compared. The comparison was counting orders. But a table that orders three times has not given three independent opinions — it has given one, three times.

Here is a dataset built to contain exactly no effect: every table spends thirty marks, whichever group it is in. One side splits it across three rounds.

Two more defects sat alongside it, and all three failed in the same direction — making the app look better than it was.

Nothing is reported until the split, the statistical power and the service guardrails all pass. A broken experiment is not a caveat printed under a number — it means there is no number. And the money figure quotes the low end of the range, so the true value is probably better than the page claims.

The trading day found at 01:35

Two hours of takings that no drawer count ever saw

Closing the day picked the date in Sarajevo. Counting the takings picked it in UTC. Two hours apart in summer — which for a café that serves past midnight is not a rounding error, it is the end of every shift.

Found because six tests began failing at 01:35 in the morning and passed again by 09:00. That is not a flaky test. That is the bug reporting itself, once a day, for two hours.

There is now one definition of the day, used on both sides of every comparison. It also fixed something nobody had reported: at 02:00, mid-service, every waiter silently lost their section and new tables stopped being assigned.

Closed days keep the snapshots they were written with — a signed-off day is a record, not a thing to rewrite. If a past close shows a surplus roughly equal to a night’s after-midnight takings, this is why.

The ranker what actually got smarter

It reads the table, not the cart

Before scoring anything, the engine asks what this table has ordered, what has actually been served, how long ago, what it has spent so far and how many people are sitting there. Then it scores, and only then does it apply the rules that can veto.

A live bug, not a hypothesis

Diet used to be inferred from the cart and applied as a hard filter. One phone at a table of four, somebody adds a salad, and every meat dish was hidden from everybody. A shared device must never speak for the whole table. An inferred preference is now a ranking penalty; only a guest’s explicit filter can remove a dish.

Underneath all of it sits a decision ledger: every ranking writes down what it considered, what it chose, and a no_suggestion row when it chose nothing — without which the denominator is unknowable. Sightings are recorded server-side and deduped; “accepted” means paid for, not tapped.
